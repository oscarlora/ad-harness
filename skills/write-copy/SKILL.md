# Write copy

Write direct response. Every ad answers fast: **what is this**, **who is it for**, **why should they care**.

## Principles

- **Use the customer's language and the user's creative direction.** Take wording from the user's brief, the persona's VOC quotes, verified reviews (`inputs/reviews.csv`) or the research doc's Reviews analysis. A brief alone is enough to build her situation; don't swap it for product facts when VOC is missing. Keep distinctive phrasing ("aches and pains", not "bounce back") in fresh lines. User wording is direction, not a testimonial or evidence for a claim.
- **Write to one reader:** the locked persona as "you", about her day. Never about the brand or "people".
- **Outcome-led, not descriptive.** The hook names the result she gets, not a feature: "Make it to 5 without the crash", not "steady energy from real oats". The visual and subline carry the mechanism.
- **Feeling first, backing second.** The number, spec or study comes right after the outcome, never as the opener.
- **Grounded in her day.** Specific situations, stakes and benefits she can picture, tied to what the product does: "You work hard. You train hard. Lunch shouldn't be another thing to figure out." Cut vague mood lines ("fits real life"), keep the emotion.
- One ad, **one angle**. Active voice.
- **Extremely simple.** Anyone gets it on one half-attentive listen: everyday words, one idea per sentence. A technical term needs what it means for her right after it ("CoQ10. Your body makes less of it as you get older").
- **Natural, speech-like flow.** Read it aloud; if it sounds like an announcer, rewrite. On-creative lines: short (under ~13 words), full sentences, no staccato fragments, no fillers. Spoken lines: how a person talks, sentences joined by *and*, *so*, *because*, *then*, a light filler only where a real speaker would ("so yeah"; never "honestly"), never stacked. No rhetorical-question fragments ("The crazy part?"); a full question the next line answers is fine, and so is a one-word reveal after a setup ("Magnesium!").
- **No redundancy**, including with the product: don't restate what the packaging already shows (protein grams, free-from chips).

## Hard rules (non-negotiable)

- **No em dashes.** Anywhere.
- **No dot separators** (·, •, |) joining items on one line. Write a sentence, or give each item its own line or slot.
- On an image ad: **max 1 hook, max 1 subline.** Tertiary text may have multiple short items.

## Match the reader's awareness

The persona's awareness stage (in the product's `-personas.md`) sets the hook's job and how much the ad explains:

- **Unaware** — open with the situation or a surprising reframe; the product arrives last; explain the most.
- **Problem-aware** — open on the pain or the real reason it keeps happening; the product is the fix.
- **Solution-aware** — open on what makes this one work, or how it beats the usual fix.
- **Product-aware** — open on proof or the strongest differentiator; keep copy short.
- **Most-aware** — open on the offer; shortest copy of all.

The market's sophistication stage (1–5, research doc's Market section) sets the claim type: stages 1–2, plain benefit claims; 3–4, lead with how the product works; 5, sell identity and experience.

The product is always in the image; how loudly the words push it follows the awareness stage.

## Hooks

A hook leads with one deliberate **writing style** (below) that makes the message instantly clear: what this is, who it's for, why they should care. Commit to a distinct style; never blur the message for cleverness or flatten every hook into one plain register.

**Volume before choosing.** Draft 10–15 hooks for the locked angle across at least four styles before showing any; synonym swaps aren't variety. Kill those that fail the tests below, then score the rest. A strong hook hits several of: curiosity pull · names her situation · a concrete outcome · a specific number, timeframe or named thing · plain words · a reason to believe · how fast results come. Show the best 3 (Output).

**Cold test, image covered:** from the words alone, would a stranger know **who** it's for (her age or life stage, plus her pain or wanted outcome) and **what** it's about (the angle)? "Finally, a protein bar with ingredients you can actually pronounce" passes; "Check the ingredients, you'll recognize every one" fails. The first product mention is **brand + plain category** ("the Acme Focus protein bar"), never a bare sub-brand.

**Local businesses:** name the verified service area clearly (video: spoken or on screen, ideally near the start; image: visible). Not in every hook; vary its placement. Never rely on scenery or targeting alone, or name a whole municipality when coverage is only part of it.

**So-what test:** a question, open loop or tease is half a message; a subline, tertiary or visual must pay it off, or the hook must be self-complete.

**Tease the answer, don't give it:** tie the solution to her pain or wanted benefit and hold back the result for the body. "Scientists tested a scoop of greens against a daily multivitamin for the nutrients busy parents run low on" (not "...and the greens came out way ahead").

**Start at the strong part:** no warm-ups ("Introducing…", "Meet…").

**No market statistics** ("1 in 3 adults…") in hooks. A number earns its place only when it names her outcome.

**Name what makes it work, don't teach it:** "the oat that digests slow"; save the how-and-why for the subline or visual.

Every supporting piece (script body, subline, tertiary) follows the hook's style and idea.

### Writing styles

One per ad: observational · rhetorical question · conditional · affirmative · contrarian · confessional · imperative · comparative · hypothetical · provocative · testimonial · declarative · conversational · cautionary · reframe · negation stack · POV · anecdotal · stat-led · definitional · quoted · permission-granting · threshold · direct address. Invent more when useful. Never vague aspiration alone: tie it to a concrete situation or benefit.

## Supporting pieces

Budgets, as guidance: a hook at most ~12 words, a subline ~20, a tertiary item 2–6.

- **Subline** — pays off the hook: usually the plain why-it-works, or the outcome made real. A benefit goes in a scene she can feel ("still full at the 3pm meeting"), not adjectives.
- **Tertiary** and every other small text slot (callouts, pills, badges, labels, list items) — one job each, no two slots the same job. The jobs: who it's for · proof (a verified customer's words, or a number with a timeframe) · the top objection, answered · offer or guarantee · how it works (ingredient or spec → what it does).
- **Review quotes**, anywhere — verbatim from `inputs/reviews.csv`, verified reviews only, never incentivized. Trim with an ellipsis, never reword.
- **Call to action / offer text** (on screen and end cards) — short, starts with a verb, always carries value (offer, guarantee, outcome): "Tap to try it at up to 40% off", never a bare "Shop now". Urgency only when real ("ends Sunday").

## Video scripts

One row per idea. Every line is visualizable and hands off to the next (a connector, cause or callback), so it plays as one story. Take the speaking style from the reference video.

- **The arc** (beats reorderable): the hook → the problem in her words (specific, a little fed up) → why it isn't her fault: who brushed her off ("it's just aging") or an outside cause she can be angry at, backed by a verifiable fact → the discovery, step by step down to one named root cause she can picture ("the first domino is a hormone called progesterone") → the product → proof as a realistic results timeline from the store's claims and verified reviews → the outcome as lived scenes that call back each pain → the CTA.
- **Bring the product in through her story, never as a cut to a spec sheet.** Name the answer as a thing first, then why she wouldn't get it the usual way, then the product as the easy way: "I kept landing on the same thing: greens. I'm not about to eat three cups of kale before work though, so I found this greens powder from Acme." Read the line before into it aloud; if it doesn't pick up, rewrite. Only the 2–3 product points that matter most for this angle and persona, most relevant first, worded fresh; never the full ingredient list or a past script's product lines.
- **The 3 hooks are alternates for one body.** Each leads naturally into Body 1: a fresh bridge per script, or straight into her story; never a past script's opener ("Let me back up"). Body 1 repeats nothing a hook says (age, symptom, the tease): read each hook into it aloud. Hooks may share one opener ("Here's an unpopular opinion:"); at least one names her symptoms in her words ("if your doctor said your night sweats, brain fog and weight gain are 'just aging'"). Short, pain- or benefit-led, spoken, never a slogan.
- **Spoken CTA:** short, closes the story, ideally calling back the hook ("So before you buy another multivitamin, try this first."). Offer, guarantee and "link below" go on the end card unless the user writes them in; a spoken price dates the video.
- **Length:** about 4 spoken words a second (40 s ≈ 160 words), the one pace for every voiceover and dialogue in the harness; on rendered dialogue about 3.25 drags and 5.35 rushes and drops words. Give the word count and seconds with every draft and revision, and flag when it runs past the target.
- **Creator (UGC) scripts:** every line gets an action, written to the creator ("Lean into the camera like you're telling a secret"). B-roll is a separate list, each shot tied to its line and matching its exact words; a problem shot gets its payoff later. Mark shots the editor sources from stock so the creator skips them. When a line changes, update its action, on-screen text, B-roll and props with it.
- **Options:** rank the 3 by relevance, lead each with a different beat, and show each in full. From a reference, keep its structure, shots and on-screen text style and vary the wording; for a new approach to it, keep its beats and change the framing. When proposing persona-angle-script pairs, prefer a persona the last few scripts didn't use, unless the reference clearly fits one.
- **User-written lines:** revise only grammar and flow. Keep their words and order, fill only their bracketed placeholders, and raise any claim the research doesn't support once, in chat, never by rewording. If they keep it, it stays.

## Claims

Every claim, anywhere in an ad:

- **Check the exact factual claim.** Product facts and efficacy need backing in the research or verified reviews; never invent numbers, mechanisms, results or testimonials. A customer's desire ("I want to perform at work") isn't a product promise ("this improves performance"); check the meaning in context, visuals included. Flag the unsupported clause, not the whole angle.
- **Keep the selling force.** When correcting a claim, change only the unsupported part; keep the stakes, tension, specificity and customer voice. Don't add "estimated" or "may help" unless truth needs it, and keep a qualifier on the fact it qualifies. User-written lines: Video scripts. If a revision reads like a catalog, rewrite it around the approved premise and stakes.
- Never guarantee results. No before/after framing. Never tell the reader something about herself ("your gut is inflamed"): Meta rejects it.
- Health-adjacent products: comfort-and-routine words, never treatment words (no "heals", "cures", "treats").
- **Sensitive subjects (animals as food, predation):** frame it as nature's wisdom. No hunting, killing, prey, blood or carcass words or footage.
- **Sell the better day, not the disease.** Poke the pain lightly; paint the after vividly.

## Output

For every piece (hook, subline, tertiary set), options differ in angle or style. Label each hook's writing style so supporting pieces can match it.
