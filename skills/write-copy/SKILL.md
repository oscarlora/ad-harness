# Write copy

Write direct response. Every ad answers three things fast: **what is this**, **who is it for**, **why should they care**.

## Principles

- **Use the customer's language and the user's creative direction.** Draw pain, desire, and outcome wording from the user's brief, the persona's VOC quotes, verified reviews (`inputs/reviews.csv`), or the research doc's Reviews analysis. A supplied brief is enough to develop the persona's situation and conflict; don't replace it with product facts because matching VOC is missing. Keep distinctive phrasing ("recovery" and "aches and pains", not an invented "bounce back"), but write fresh lines that develop the brief naturally. User-supplied wording guides the creative; it is not a verified testimonial or evidence for a product claim.
- **Write to one reader.** Every line speaks to the locked persona as "you", about her day. Never about the brand, never about "people".
- **Outcome-led, not descriptive.** Lead with the result the persona gets — what changes in her day — not a description of the product or a feature. "Make it to 5 without the crash" (outcome) beats "steady energy from real oats" (feature). State the benefit in the hook; let the visual and subline carry the mechanism. Test every hook: does it name a *result the reader wants*, or just describe the product? If the latter, rewrite.
- **Feeling first, backing second.** Lead with the outcome the reader wants; bring the number, spec, or study right after as backing, never as the opener.
- **Direct response, grounded in her day.** Use specific situations, desires, stakes, and benefits the reader can picture. Her ambition and frustration give the ad a reason to matter; connect them to what the product actually does. "You work hard. You train hard. Lunch shouldn't be another thing to figure out" carries a concrete tension without promising better performance. Rewrite vague mood statements ("fits real life", "everything after"), not the emotion that makes her care.
- One ad communicates **one angle**.
- Active voice.
- **Extremely simple.** Write so anyone gets it on one listen, half paying attention: everyday words, one idea per sentence, nothing she has to stop and decode. A technical term (a nutrient, a hormone, a study) earns its place only with what it means for her, in plain words, right after it ("CoQ10. Your body makes less of it as you get older"). If a simpler word says the same thing, use it.
- **Natural, speech-like flow.** Read every line aloud: if it sounds like an ad announcer instead of a friend talking, rewrite it. On-creative text stays short (past roughly 13 words a line starts losing people), but short means full natural sentences, not punchy staccato fragments, and no fillers. Spoken lines (video scripts) go further: write exactly how a person talks, in full sentences that flow into each other with *and*, *so*, *because*, *then*, with light fillers only where a real speaker would use them ("so yeah", "literally"; never "honestly"), never stacked. No rhetorical-question fragments ("And my husband?", "The crazy part?"): just say the thing. A full question to the viewer that the next line answers is fine ("And do you know what happens when we run low on iron?"), and so is a one-word reveal after a setup ("Magnesium!").
- No redundancy. If one element already says it, don't repeat it elsewhere — including the product itself: don't restate claims already legible on the wrapper (protein grams, ingredient count, free-from chips).

## Hard rules (non-negotiable)

- **No em dashes.** Anywhere.
- **No dot separators** (·, •, |) joining items on one line. Write a sentence, or give each item its own line or slot.
- On an image ad: **max 1 hook, max 1 subline.** Tertiary text may have multiple short items.

## Match the reader's awareness

The persona's awareness stage (in the product's `-personas.md`) sets the hook's job and how much the ad explains:

- **Unaware** — open with the situation or a surprising reframe; the product arrives last; the ad explains the most.
- **Problem-aware** — open on the pain or the real reason it keeps happening; the product is the fix.
- **Solution-aware** — open on what makes this one work, or how it beats the usual fix.
- **Product-aware** — open on proof or the strongest differentiator; keep copy short.
- **Most-aware** — open on the offer; shortest copy of all.

The market's stage (the 1–5 sophistication stage in the research doc's Market section) sets the claim type: in a fresh market (stages 1–2) plain benefit claims land; in a crowded one (3–4), lead with how the product works; in an exhausted one (5), sell identity and experience.

The product is always in the image; how loudly the words push it follows the awareness stage.

## Hooks

A hook leads with a deliberate **writing style** (see below), and that style is what makes the message instantly clear: **what this is, who it's for, and why they should care**, shaped by the angle and in the customer's words. The style carries the voice and the variety; clarity is the job it has to do. Pick a distinct style and commit to it, but never at the message's expense: don't blur it for cleverness, and don't flatten every hook into the same plain register to be clear.

**Volume before choosing.** Never keep the first draft. For the locked angle, draft 10–15 hooks across at least four writing styles before showing anything. Range from direct to situation-led to unexpected framings; changing synonyms in the same sentence template isn't variety. Kill the ones that fail the tests below, then score the survivors — a strong hook hits several of: curiosity pull · names the reader's situation · a concrete outcome · a specific number, timeframe, or named thing · plain words · a reason to believe · how fast results come. The best 3 become the options you show (Output below).

**Test each hook cold, with the image covered:** from the words alone, would a stranger know who it is for and what it is about? Every hook signals **who** (the persona's age or life stage, plus her pain or the outcome she wants) and **what** (the locked angle), in the words themselves, never leaning on the image. For local or geographically limited businesses, the ad must clearly name the verified service area: in video, spoken or on-screen somewhere in the video, ideally near the start; in images, clearly visible in the creative. Location filters for people who can actually buy, but needn't be in every hook: vary its placement and creative treatment to serve the story. Don't rely on scenery or paid targeting alone, and don't name a whole municipality when coverage is only part of it. (e.g. "Check the ingredients, you'll recognize every one" fails it — what product, for whom? "Finally, a protein bar with ingredients you can actually pronounce" passes.) The ad is for a cold audience: the first mention of the product is always **brand + plain category** ("the Acme Focus protein bar"), never a bare sub-brand or model name ("the Focus") the reader can't place.

**Then the so-what test:** does the hook still leave the reader thinking "so what?" A question, open loop, or teased tension is only half a message; it must be paid off by a subline, tertiary, or supporting visual. If nothing resolves it, either make the hook self-complete (put the payoff in the hook itself) or add the element that delivers it.

**Tease the answer, don't give it:** tie the solution or comparison to the persona's pain or the benefit she wants, and hold back the result so the body has something to pay off. "Scientists tested a scoop of greens against a daily multivitamin for the nutrients busy parents run low on" beats "...and the greens came out way ahead".

**Start at the strong part:** no warm-up words ("Introducing…", "Let's talk about…", "Meet…") — the first word already carries the message.

**One reader, not a population:** market statistics ("1 in 3 adults…") are banned in hooks — the reader cares about her own day. A number earns its place only when it names her outcome, not the market's. (The stat-led style still exists; this rule decides which stats qualify.)

**Name what makes it work, don't teach it:** say the working part in customer words ("the oat that digests slow") and save the full how-and-why for the subline or the visual. No chemistry lessons in a hook.

The hook is written in **one writing style**, and every supporting piece (script body, subline, tertiary) follows that same style and idea.

> Example — observational style. Hook: "Most women think stress is just part of life". The subline/body continues that observational voice and pays it off.

### Writing styles

Pick one per ad and keep it consistent: observational · rhetorical question · conditional · affirmative · contrarian · confessional · imperative · comparative · hypothetical · provocative · testimonial · declarative · conversational · cautionary · reframe · negation stack · POV · anecdotal · stat-led · definitional · quoted · permission-granting · threshold · direct address.

(No vague aspiration-only copy: identity and ambition must connect to a concrete situation or benefit.)

Not fixed — invent more when useful.

## Supporting pieces

Budgets, as guidance: a hook is at most ~12 words (past that it starts being a subline), a subline at most ~20, a tertiary item 2–6 words.

- **Subline** — delivers what the hook promised or teased: usually the plain one-line why-it-works, or the outcome made real. When it carries a benefit, set it in a scene she can feel (time, place, sense: "still full at the 3pm meeting"), not adjectives.
- **Tertiary**, and every other small text slot a format places (callouts, pills, badges, stamps, list items, table cells, labels) — each slot does exactly one job, and no two slots repeat a job. The jobs: who it's for · proof (a verified customer's words, or a number with a timeframe) · the top objection, answered · offer or guarantee · how it works (ingredient or spec → what it does).
- **Review quotes**, anywhere in an ad — verbatim from `inputs/reviews.csv`, verified reviews only, never incentivized ones. Trim with an ellipsis, never reword.
- **Call to action / offer text** (on screen and end cards; spoken CTAs are in Video scripts) — short, starts with a verb, and always carries value: the offer ("Tap to try it at up to 40% off"), the guarantee ("Tap to try it risk-free for 30 days"), the outcome she wants, or her pain turned into a benefit. Never a bare "Tap to shop" or "Shop now", including on video end cards. Urgency only when it's real and near ("ends Sunday"); invented scarcity never.

## Video scripts

One row per idea, every line visualizable with footage, and every line hands off to the next (a connector, a cause, or a callback), so it plays as one story rather than a list. Take the speaking style from the reference video.

- **The arc** (beats reorderable): the hook → the problem in her words (specific, lived, a little fed up) → why it isn't her fault: who brushed her off (the doctor who says her labs are normal, "it's just aging"), or an outside cause she can be angry at, backed by a fact you can verify ("until 1993, government-funded medical studies didn't even have to include women") → the discovery: the real reason, step by step down to one named root cause she can picture ("the first domino is a hormone called progesterone") → the product → proof as a realistic results timeline from the store's claims and verified reviews → the outcome as lived scenes that call back each pain → the CTA.
- **Bring the product in through her story, never as a cut to a spec sheet.** This is where scripts break most often. Name the answer as a thing first (the food, the ingredient, the habit), then why she wouldn't get it the usual way, then the product as the easy way: "I kept landing on the same thing: greens. Our grandmas were right. I'm not about to eat three cups of kale before work though, so I found this greens powder from Acme." Read the line before the product into the product line aloud; if the product line doesn't pick up where it left off, rewrite. Say only the 2–3 product points that matter most for this angle and persona, most relevant first, worded fresh; never the full ingredient list or a past script's product lines.
- **The 3 hooks are alternates for one body.** Each leads naturally into Body 1: write that bridge fresh for each script, or skip it and step straight into her story; never reuse a past script's opener ("Let me back up", "Let me explain"). Body 1 repeats no detail any hook says (age, symptom, the tease): read each hook straight into Body 1 aloud before showing it. They may share one opener ("Here's an unpopular opinion:", "Ladies, three things…") and vary what follows; at least one names her symptoms in her words so she recognizes herself ("if your doctor said your night sweats, brain fog and weight gain are 'just aging'"). Short, straight, pain- or benefit-led, and still spoken, never a slogan.
- **Spoken CTA:** short, and it closes the story, ideally calling back the hook ("So before you buy another multivitamin, try this first."). The offer, guarantee and "link below" go on the end card unless the user writes them in; a spoken price dates the video.
- **Length:** about 4 spoken words a second (40 s ≈ 160 words), the one pace for every voiceover and dialogue in the harness; on rendered dialogue about 3.25 drags and 5.35 rushes and drops words. Give the word count and seconds with every draft and revision, and flag when it runs past the target.
- **Creator (UGC) scripts:** every line gets an action, written to the creator in second person ("Lean into the camera like you're telling a secret"). B-roll is a separate list, each shot tied to the line it covers and matching that line's exact words; a problem shot gets its payoff later (sticky notes going on, then coming off). Mark shots the editor sources from stock so the creator skips them. When a line changes, update its action, on-screen text, B-roll and props in the same pass.
- **Options:** rank the 3 by relevance, lead each with a different beat, and show each in full, never only the part that changed. From a reference, keep its structure, shots and on-screen text style and vary the wording; asked for a new approach to it, keep its beats and change the framing. When proposing persona-angle-script pairs, prefer a persona the last few scripts didn't use, unless the reference clearly fits one.
- **User-written lines:** revise only grammar and flow. Keep their words, phrasing and order, fill only their bracketed placeholders, and raise any claim the research doesn't support once, in chat, never by rewording the line. If they keep it, it stays as written.

## Claims

Every claim, anywhere in an ad, follows these:

- **Check the exact factual claim.** Product facts and efficacy claims need backing in the research or verified reviews; don't invent numbers, mechanisms, results, or testimonials. A customer's desire ("I want to perform at work and in training") is not a product promise ("this meal improves mental and physical performance"); check the meaning in context, including what the visuals imply. If one clause lacks backing, flag that clause, not the whole angle.
- **Keep the selling force.** Preserve the emotional stakes, tension, specificity, and customer voice when correcting a claim; change only the unsupported part. Keep technical qualifiers attached to the fact they qualify, not spread across the creative: don't add "estimated", "may help", or "suitable for your goals" unless needed for truthful meaning. For user-written lines, follow the rule in Video scripts: flag unsupported wording separately in chat and offer a targeted alternative, never silently soften their copy. Before showing a revision, check that the approved premise, conflict, and motivation still drive the story and give this reader a compelling reason to buy; if it reads like a product catalog, rewrite around those stakes.
- Never guarantee results. No before/after framing. Never tell the reader something about herself ("your gut is inflamed") — Meta rejects it and it reads wrong.
- Health-adjacent products: comfort-and-routine words, never treatment words (no "heals", "cures", "treats").
- **Sensitive subjects (animals as food, predation):** frame it as nature's wisdom, the way things are for a reason. No hunting, killing, prey, blood, or carcass words or footage.
- **Sell the better day, not the disease.** On Meta, positive framing outperforms and survives ad review. Poke the pain lightly; paint the after vividly.

## Output

For every piece (hook, subline, tertiary set), give **3 options, deliberately differentiated** in angle of attack or style so the user sees real variety. Label which writing style each hook uses so supporting pieces can match it.
